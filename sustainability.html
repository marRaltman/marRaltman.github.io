What I Saw on the Island

I spent June studying abroad in Puerto Rico, and it reshaped how I think about sustainability, tourism, and who gets to decide what "development" means for a place. Snorkeling reef surveys off the southwest coast and sitting in on lectures about coral bleaching made the science real in a way a classroom never could — but it was the conversations with local farmers and fishers that stuck with me most.

I visited small-scale, regenerative farms working to restore soil health after decades of monoculture agriculture, and talked with cooperatives experimenting with agroecology as a way to build food sovereignty independent of imported goods. I also saw the flip side: coastline being sold off for resorts and short-term rentals, pricing out the very communities whose stewardship kept those ecosystems intact in the first place.

That contrast is what pulled me toward the island's political history. Puerto Rico's environmental and economic struggles are inseparable from its colonial status — from the Jones Act's shipping restrictions to fiscal oversight boards making decisions with no local vote. Learning about grassroots pushes for legislative reform, energy independence, and decolonization gave me language for something I'd only felt instinctively: you can't talk about "sustainability" honestly without talking about sovereignty.

"You can't preserve what you don't control." — something a farmer I met outside Cabo Rojo said, and I haven't stopped thinking about it since.

I came home wanting to keep learning — about marine ecosystems, about regenerative agriculture, and about the anti-colonial legislation and organizing that treats environmental justice and political self-determination as the same fight.
